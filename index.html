Live Seattle 911 Response-Time Prediction Pipeline

A real-time Azure system that predicts how long emergency responders will take to arrive the moment a new 911 call comes in.

The problem

In life-threatening emergencies, survival odds drop about 10% for every minute of delay, yet Seattle dispatch relies on static historical averages. Seattle police average about 10 minutes to respond, but individual response times range from under a minute to nearly an hour. Traffic, weather, and distance from the station all play a role. If dispatchers could see a likely delay before it happens, they could allocate units better.

The data

  • Seattle Police CAD data: 43,500+ historical 911 incidents from August 2024, with response time in seconds as the target. These served as a proxy for fire-department response.
  • TomTom Traffic API: average and minimum route speed and a relative-speed ratio for the stretch between each incident and its fire station. The API is called once per 911 call to keep costs down.
  • OpenWeather API: temperature, wind, and conditions at the time of each call.

Architecture

IngestTimer-triggered Function pulls the live Seattle Fire 911 feed into Event Hubs
EnrichEvent-triggered Function calls TomTom. Stream Analytics joins traffic and weather.
PredictFunction loads the pickled Random Forest and scores each enriched event
ServePredictions land in the ADLS gold layer and feed Power BI

Data moves through bronze, silver, and gold layers (a medallion architecture) in Azure Data Lake Storage Gen2, with serverless Synapse for queries. A daily evaluation Function matches each prediction to the police ground-truth incident and reports MAE, RMSE, and the share of predictions within 6 and 10 minutes.

Model

We chose a Random Forest for four reasons. Traffic features had near-zero linear correlation with response time, but a forest can still capture non-linear interactions. It reports feature importance without extra tooling. It needs no feature scaling. And it's far less thrown off than linear regression by outliers, since response times range from 1 second to 57 minutes.

The honest result: training on all 43K+ incidents gave R² ≈ 0.03 and an MAE of about 13 minutes. Response time is dominated by confounders that aren't in the data. Narrowing to fire-assistance calls (n = 108) with leave-one-out CV cut the MAE to about 4.6 minutes.
Top featureImportance
Distance to station24.7%

Engineering challenges

Fire and police data didn't share IDsWe ran inference on the live fire-department stream but trained on police history. I wrote a join based on timestamp and location (haversine distance, ±5 minutes) so predictions could be evaluated against real outcomes.
Nested weather JSONOpenWeather returns deeply nested fields, so the Stream Analytics query had to reference paths like w.[weather].[main].[temp] directly.
Traffic was averaged by weekdayOne bulk TomTom pull came back as weekday averages. We ran 14 separate daily jobs over two weeks to get real per-day traffic.
Small training setFiltering to fire-assistance calls left 108 examples, so we used leave-one-out cross-validation. Captured stream events can retrain the model with more weather variety over time.

Cost decisions

  • Consumption-tier Function Apps, which bill per execution and cost nothing when idle.
  • Serverless Synapse instead of a dedicated pool, which runs about $700+ a month.
  • scikit-learn running inside an Azure Function, with no Docker, Container Apps, or Azure ML Studio.

Projects / Offline RL Trading
May 2025DS 543 Final Projectwith Sachiel Chukrow

Offline Reinforcement Learning for Quantitative Trading

Trading agents that learn only from historical data, so a bad policy never gets tested with real money.

Why offline RL

Reinforcement learning normally improves a policy by trying actions and seeing what happens. In the stock market, that trial and error costs money. Offline RL learns from a fixed historical dataset instead. The catch is action distribution shift: the model can wildly overestimate actions the data never showed. Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) and Batch-Constrained Q-Learning (BCQ) are built to prevent that.

The environment

Each step is one trading day. The state includes stock prices, last-reported dividends, volume, the 10-year Treasury rate, cash held, and S&P 500 data, all normalized. The action space is continuous: the agent reallocates its portfolio at the end of each day.

Instead of rewarding raw portfolio growth, the reward measures performance relative to the market, with a cost for every trade so the agent learns not to churn:

reward = %Δ portfolio value − %Δ S&P 500 − (trade cost × total trades)

This means a 1% gain in a falling market counts for more than a 1% gain in a rising one.

Models

Baselines

Linear regression and a random forest tested whether simple models could call market direction. Both hit rates were roughly a coin flip. The forest "predicted" that stocks always go down, which inflated its accuracy. A DQN with a discretized action space was unstable and put most of the portfolio into a single stock.

CQL (Actor-Free)

A double-Q architecture trained on expert data from a momentum strategy, which holds the 10 stocks with the best Sharpe ratio at 95% invested. A behavior-cloning network proposes continuous actions for the Q-network to evaluate.

CQL (Actor-Critic)

Trained on random actions instead of an expert, so it isn't capped by the expert's choices. We added an entropy bonus to the actor's objective to encourage a diversified portfolio over "all eggs in one basket."

BCQ

A VAE learns which actions are plausible given the historical data, and a perturbation network nudges them slightly outside it. We compared a partially online version with a fully offline one.

Results

StrategyReturn
CQL Actor-Critic81.7%
CQL Actor-Free80.7%
Buy and hold74.7%
S&P 50065.8%

BCQ was trained on 1990–2019 and tested on 2020–2025. The fully offline version reached about 120.2%, beating the market by 5.65 points. The partially online version reached 116.45%, 1.45 points above the market. BCQ's biggest strength was damage control: given poor-performing stocks, it fell at most about 9% behind the market over five years.

Limitation: the sharp 2025 tariff-driven crash hit the model hard. How conservative agents handle crashes that fast is an open question.

Projects / Fantasy Football
Dec 2024DS 340Flask web app

Fantasy Football Player Projections with LSTMs

Type in an NFL player and get next week's fantasy projection, generated by a stacked LSTM.

What it does

The user enters a player's name in the web app. The Flask back end builds that player's recent game history, trains and runs an LSTM, and returns the projected points along with a chart of training and validation error.

Data

The dataset has weekly player stats for 50,863 player-weeks across 1,512 players from 2012 to 2023. I removed draft, physical, and team-context columns that don't describe a player's on-field production. Each training example is a sliding window of the player's previous 3 weeks, used to predict the next week. Data was split 90/10 into training and test sets.

How the model evolved

  1. Single dense layer as a linear baseline.
  2. Tuned dense network with batch normalization, dropout, Leaky ReLU, and learning-rate tuning. It gave only small gains.
  3. Custom capped activation that limits projections to 0–25 points. It didn't help.
  4. Random Forest baseline: MSE of 53.0.
  5. Stacked LSTM with 100, 50, and 25 units, plus dropout and early stopping, to model each player's sequence of weeks directly. This is the model the app serves.
The flat models plateaued at a validation loss around 52. Fantasy output is noisy week to week, which is why I moved to a sequence model that reads a player's recent trend.

Projects / RL Lab Work
Spring 2025DS 543 · Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement Learning Lab Work

Core RL algorithms implemented from scratch: imitation learning, exploration, and multi-agent equilibria.

Imitation learning: teaching a cheetah to run

In MuJoCo's HalfCheetah environment, I trained a behavior-cloning policy on 1,000 expert state-action pairs (a 64-64 MLP trained with MSE loss for 2,000 epochs). I then trained a second policy with DAgger, which rolls out the learner, asks the expert what it would have done at each state it visited, and retrains on the combined data.

PolicyMean rewardStd. dev.
Untrained−1.80.7
Behavior cloning3,637796
DAgger3,803157

DAgger's main win was consistency. It cut the variance between episodes by about 80% because it learned how to recover from states the expert never visited.

Exploration: the combination lock

I built a combination-lock environment with 10 actions per step, where only one action at each step keeps you on the good path. The reward comes only at the end, after up to 20 steps. On that environment I compared:

  • UCBVI: tabular value iteration with a count-based optimism bonus, α/√N(s,a), with α tuned.
  • DQN with ε-greedy: random exploration with tuned decay schedules.
  • DQN with Random Network Distillation: an intrinsic reward from how badly a predictor network matches a fixed random network on a state.

Multi-agent: two-player blackjack

I built blackjack as a two-player, zero-sum Markov game with 1,764 states and solved it with Nash value iteration, computing a 2×2 matrix-game equilibrium at each state. Some states produce mixed strategies. For example, at a soft 6 against an opponent's 13, the equilibrium has player 1 stand about 77% of the time.

Projects / Stranger Danger
Spring 2025DS 549 · BU Spark!Team of 3

Identifying Children's "Stranger Danger" Behaviors with Computer Vision

A web app that turns days of manual video coding into a 3-minute automated analysis, built for a BU developmental psychology lab studying early risk factors for anxiety.

The problem

Behavioral inhibition, a child's tendency toward fear and withdrawal in new situations, is an early predictor of anxiety later in life. Researchers study it with "Stranger Danger" experiments: a stranger enters the room, and the child's reaction is recorded on video.

Our client, Kathy Sem, a PhD researcher in BU's Biobehavioral and Social-Emotional Development (BASE) Lab, had 600+ videos from BU and Penn waiting to be coded. Coding one video by hand took multiple days and needed at least two reviewers, which stalled the research.

What the app outputs

For every second of video, the app predicts the three behaviors the lab codes:

  • Proximity: how close the child is to the caregiver or the stranger (0–2 scale)
  • Fear: how much fear the child shows (0–2 scale)
  • Freezing: whether the child freezes, flagged when fear lasts 2+ seconds

How it works

  1. Detection: a fine-tuned YOLO model finds the child, parent, and stranger in each frame.
  2. Feature extraction: pose estimation measures the child's movement, facial-movement tracking catches expression changes, and depth estimation gives the distance to each adult.
  3. Baseline: the video is split into free play and the experiment. Free-play movement sets a per-child baseline, so experiment movement is scored as a ratio.
  4. Classification: three decision-tree classifiers, trained on the lab's labels after converting them from start/stop ranges to second-by-second labels, predict proximity, fear, and freezing.
  5. Web app: upload a video with one click. It's cropped, processed, and labeled in a single workflow, and the app is deployed on Hugging Face Spaces.
Constraint we designed around: labeled data arrived late in the project. We built feature extraction and pilot models first, so the classifiers could scale up once labels came in.

Projects / NHANES Sleep
2026BA 305Team of 3

Predicting Sleep Behavior from Lifestyle Data

Can lifestyle predict how much people sleep, or whether they sleep badly? An analysis of about 14,000 CDC survey respondents.

The data

We merged several components of the CDC's National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2011 to 2018 on participant ID, giving about 14,000 cleaned records. We trimmed 632 columns to 69, keeping sleep, depression, physical activity, demographics, body measures, diet, smoking, alcohol, and medical conditions. We also decoded NHANES conventions: codes like 7, 9, 77, and 99 mean "don't know" or "refused," and many nulls actually mean zero.

Question 1: Can we predict sleep hours?

No. We tuned decision trees (27 hyperparameter combinations, 5-fold CV), random forests, and neural networks with 1–5 layers. Every model stayed below 4% R². The best was a random forest with RMSE 1.466 hours and R² ≈ 3.6%. The neural nets collapsed to predicting the mean. PCA didn't help either: it needed 42 of 69 components to reach 90% of the variance.

Takeaway: sleep duration is largely a choice, and these lifestyle features don't capture it.

Question 2: Can we predict sleep problems?

Yes, moderately. Multi-class severity models (k-NN and random forest) did worse than always guessing the majority class. Reframing the problem as binary, any sleep problem vs. none, fixed the class imbalance. Of four tuned models, XGBoost performed best:

ModelAccuracyMacro F1
XGBoost (all features)0.7520.741
XGBoost (no depression variables)—0.60

Depression-survey answers were the strongest predictors, consistent with the two-way link between sleep and depression. With those removed, smoking, alcohol, and pre-existing conditions rose to the top. Mann-Whitney U tests confirmed that people with sleep problems smoke and drink more (p ≈ 10⁻³⁴), though the real-world gap is small, around half a drink a day.

Projects / Kalshi Edge Finder
Fall 2026Personal projectPython · FastAPI

Kalshi vs. Sportsbook Edge Finder

An automated app that compares Kalshi's prediction-market prices to sportsbooks' fair odds and flags contracts that are underpriced after fees. It's read-only and never places a trade.

The idea

Sportsbooks set sharp prices but build in a margin. Kalshi lists contracts on the same games. If you remove the books' margin and treat their consensus as the "true" probability, any Kalshi contract priced below that, after Kalshi's fee, is a potential edge.

How it works

  1. Fair price: pulls odds from The Odds API, removes each book's margin (power-method de-vig), and takes a weighted consensus with Pinnacle weighted 3×.
  2. Match: pairs each game with Kalshi's public market data across NFL, NBA, WNBA, NHL, and MLB moneylines, plus spreads and totals with sport-specific line matching.
  3. Score: net edge = fair − price − fee, using Kalshi's taker fee formula, ceil(0.07 × C × P × (1 − P)).
  4. Cost to fill: on click, it walks Kalshi's live order book level by level to show average price, slippage, and edge after all costs.
  5. Track honestly: paper trades, a convergence panel showing whether Kalshi drifts toward the books after a flag, and SQLite snapshots, since no free Kalshi price history exists for backtesting.

Engineering for a tight API budget

The free Odds API tier gives 500 credits a month, about 16 a day, shared across every sport. The scheduler rations them automatically: a sport is polled every 30 minutes when its next game is within 3 hours, every 90 minutes within 12 hours, and never beyond that. A rolling 24-hour cap and an untouchable 25-credit reserve keep it inside budget. Kalshi data is free and refreshes every 20 seconds, or every 3 seconds in Live mode.

Built-in skepticism: free-tier book data can be 30–90 minutes old, so many "gaps" are just Kalshi reacting to news first. The app is meant to be judged on its paper-trade and convergence results, not a good weekend.

Frequency of Blunt Cerebrovascular Injury in Strangulation Survivors

A retrospective study of patients who survived strangulation at a Level 1 trauma center. I led the data analysis as a ChristianaCare data analyst intern.

Why it matters

Blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI), damage to the arteries in the neck, is easy to miss after strangulation. Half of patients show no visible injuries, and strokes can appear days later. Screening guidelines exist, but how often BCVI actually occurs in survivors screened by those criteria had not been established.

The study

We reviewed 306 patients evaluated for strangulation from 2015 to 2018, entering 69 data points per patient from both forensic nurse and physician documentation. Then we compared which assessments met the Training Institute for Strangulation Prevention's criteria for CT angiography.

Findings

Redlining in the Numbers

Modern redlining has been found in over 61 U.S. metro areas, even after controlling for applicant factors. This paper tests whether it appears in Washington State's 2016 mortgage data.

Data

The Home Mortgage Disclosure Act requires lenders to publish loan-level records, including applicant demographics, income, and the outcome. I analyzed the 2016 Washington State HMDA dataset with pandas and statsmodels, focusing on conventional mortgage applications.

Finding 1: Black applicants are denied more often

GroupApprovedDenied
All applicants92.25%7.75%
Black applicants87.74%12.26%
All other applicants92.34%7.66%

A z-test for difference in proportions confirmed the gap is statistically significant. A higher denial rate alone doesn't prove discrimination, though, so the next step was to look at why people were denied.

Finding 2: The reasons differ

If lenders treated groups the same, the reasons for denial should be distributed similarly. Most were, except one:

Denial reasonBlackOthers
Credit history37.1%26.1%
Debt-to-income ratio34.7%31.7%
Collateral14.9%19.5%
Application incomplete11.2%15.7%
Insufficient cash7.6%6.4%

The credit-history gap was also statistically significant. An application can list up to three reasons, so the columns don't add to 100%.

The argument

The dataset doesn't include credit scores, so I used applicant income as a check on whether finances alone explain the gap. The paper argues the bias reaches further back than the loan decision. When historical exclusion keeps Black families out of the systems that build credit, "credit history" becomes a race-correlated filter that looks neutral. Fixing the loan approval step alone won't close the gap.

Writing / The FTIA
Dec 2024DS 380Policy proposal

Addressing Racial Bias in the Financial System: The Financial Transparency and Inclusion Act

Current fair-lending laws encourage equal access but don't enforce it. This paper proposes a federal act that does.

The problem

Many of today's financial inequities trace back to the Home Owners' Loan Corporation, whose maps labeled minority neighborhoods "high risk" and institutionalized redlining. Today there are 35% fewer bank branches in predominantly Black neighborhoods. In 2015, 15% of Black Americans were "credit invisible," with no usable credit history, compared with 9% of white Americans. Each disadvantage feeds the next: no credit history means no mortgage, which means no home equity, which means no generational wealth.

Existing laws leave gaps. The Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) only encourages banks to serve their communities. The Fair Access to Financial Services Act bans discrimination but doesn't undo biased infrastructure. HMDA data isn't detailed enough to confirm bias.

The proposal

  1. Give the CRA teeth. Banks that comply get tax benefits, priority access to federal lending programs, and faster merger approvals. Banks that don't face fines, required corrective action plans, and blocked mergers.
  2. A CRA for technology. Tech companies reinvest in underserved areas through broadband expansion and online-banking literacy programs, funded by public-private partnerships, subsidies, and tax credits.
  3. Fix the inputs. Credit evaluations count rent and utility payments, which are more consistent across people than traditional credit lines. Institutions must be transparent about data-driven credit decisions so algorithmic bias can be diagnosed.

Trade-offs

Cost: new branches and more data reporting are expensive. The paper argues that long-term gains for communities and new business for banks outweigh the upfront investment.

Privacy: more data raises breach risk. Shared data would stay anonymized as HMDA data already is and align with CCPA standards. Banks already hold rent and utility payment data because they process those transactions.
